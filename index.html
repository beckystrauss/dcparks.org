Building Tomorrow’s Parks

Recommendations from
The DC Neighborhood Parks Task Force
Photo: Sam Kittner

The District is fortunate to have some of the most widely distributed green space of any U.S. city. But about 90 percent of it is managed by the National Park Service, which was built to protect national treasures, not run neighborhood playgrounds.

Managing and caring for DC’s parks is overly complicated, with responsibilities spread across several federal and local agencies. The result is uneven quality and parks that aren’t responsive to dynamic local needs.

The Task Force report lays out a path to give DC residents better neighborhood parks—with more local control, agency coordination, and community stewardship.

The Task Force brought together civic, community, and business leaders from all eight wards, including ANC commissioners, Business Improvement Districts, community-based organizations, parks and recreation experts, and parks advocates, with technical input from DC agency staff.

The recommendations

Four steps toward a world-class park system

Actions the Task Force hopes DC’s next Mayor will pursue with the Council, communities, and the federal government.

01

Transfer locally serving parks from NPS to the District

The District and NPS should jointly assess which parks are best managed locally and which belong under federal stewardship, based on use, significance, and management needs.

  • Parks that primarily serve local residents should be managed by the District.
  • Federal resources should focus on nationally significant parks and assets.
  • Historic and cultural assets stay protected through easements or other mechanisms.
  • Start with remnants, small parks, and parks that can draw on outside partners.
02

Build District park management and coordination capacity

The District’s capacity to manage parks will need to grow significantly to support an expanded local parks portfolio.

  • Study how to organize park planning, management, programming, maintenance, and capital functions.
  • Explore a consolidated agency or public entity.
  • Create a Coordinator of the Public Realm to work across District agencies, NPS, and partners.
  • Provide additional funding for citywide park planning and implementation.
Model: New York City’s Chief Public Realm Officer
03

Explore sustainable park financing and cost recovery

DC recovers less than 5 percent of its parks and recreation costs, compared to a national average of 26 percent. A new framework can raise revenue while keeping access open to everyone.

  • Increase cost recovery from programs and facilities with individualized benefits.
  • Keep programs with broad community benefit subsidized or free, with reductions for low-income residents.
  • Establish an Entrepreneurial Parks Fund for sponsorships, concessions, and advertising.
  • Reduce costs through operational efficiencies and expand partnerships with BIDs and community groups.
04

Foster community management of small neighborhood parks

The District should leverage community-based stewardship to supplement public management, strengthen community connections to parks, and make green spaces more responsive to neighborhood needs.

  • Engage residents, neighborhood organizations, ANCs, and other local partners in designing, managing, and caring for neighborhood parks.
  • Partner with intermediary partners to provide insurance, fiscal sponsorship, technical assistance, coordination with District agencies, and ongoing operational support as community leadership and capacity evolve.
  • Collaborate with Casey Trees to pilot and expand stewardship opportunities across all eight wards, particularly for small neighborhood parks and green spaces.
Model: Chicago’s NeighborSpace

Key principles

What guided the Task Force

  1. Manage parks to create environmental, public health, community safety, and economic value for neighborhoods and residents in every part of the District.
  2. Allocate park resources so that every resident has access to high-quality, safe, and welcoming parks.
  3. Be as responsive as possible to the needs of each neighborhood.
  4. Keep park land dedicated to public use and enjoyment.
  5. Pursue partnerships to reduce or eliminate the public cost of managing small neighborhood parks while increasing local access to outdoor space.
  6. Create administrative efficiencies that reduce the city’s park management costs and improve outcomes.
  7. Improve customer service so residents can easily reserve amenities, report problems, suggest improvements, and navigate the parks system.
